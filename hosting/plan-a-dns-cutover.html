Plan a DNS cutover using the old record’s TTL

Estimate when a resolver may stop returning the previous address and use a separate check to confirm which deployment answers.

Lilik HartantiPublished 27 November 2026

At 10:00 you change a working web record. Your phone opens the new homepage and a colleague still sees the old one. Both observations can be accurate. The useful question is when each resolver cached the old answer and which time to live it received.

Follow one resolver’s clock

Suppose a resolver looked up the old address at 09:55 and was given 3,600 seconds. A routine positive cache entry can remain usable there until about 10:55. Editing the authoritative record at 10:00 gives later lookups a new answer. It cannot reach back into that resolver to replace the five-minute-old entry. A second resolver that first asks at 10:01 may learn the new address immediately.

This example assumes an existing positive answer for the same name and record type. A name that previously did not exist can have a cached negative response with a different lifetime. A nameserver migration also involves delegation caches.

Calculate the overlap before the move

Imagine the old TTL is 3,600 seconds. At 09:40 you lower it to 300 seconds while keeping the old address. You switch the address twenty minutes later. A resolver that last filled its cache just before 09:40 can still have about forty minutes left on the longer TTL. A resolver that fetched the old address just before 10:00 under the shorter TTL may retain it for about five minutes.

The longer of those two windows is the planning figure for these assumptions. If possible, wait at least the full previous TTL after the reduction before switching the address. Use the DNS cutover worksheet to compare a short lead time with a full one.

Give old and new hosts a harmless marker

Publish a small revision label on a test page, such as catalog-r1 at the old host and catalog-r2 at the new one. Verify each on its deployment address. While both sites can receive traffic, keep their public content usable. If they accept orders or edits, plan how writes will be kept consistent before the move.

Ask a colleague which label they see, together with the hostname and time. If different networks reach the new host but one tab still shows the old layout, look at browser or CDN caching too. DNS selects a service. It does not choose which revision of a stylesheet that service returns.

Decide when to retire the previous host

Use the worksheet as a planning estimate. Check the active authoritative record, repeat requests from representative networks, and look at the old host’s ordinary traffic logs if available to you. Any remaining meaningful requests deserve an explanation before you cancel service. A quick rollback changes the authoritative answer again and creates another period in which clients can disagree.
